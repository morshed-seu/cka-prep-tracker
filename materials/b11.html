    B11 — Service management: systemd, logs, boot

    Something has to be in charge. When a machine boots, the kernel starts exactly one process and hands it the entire job of turning a bare kernel into a working system: start the things that should run, in an order that works, restart them when they die, collect what they printed, and keep doing all of that until someone turns the power off. On a modern Linux box that process is systemd, and the units it manages are the vocabulary in which every service on every server you will ever touch is described. This is the building superintendent: they get the boiler running and the lights on before any tenant office can open its doors.

    The reason this module sits here, after processes and cgroups, is that a systemd unit turns out to be made entirely of things you already understand. A unit's status is a process, an exit code and a signal (B2.8) formatted into a paragraph. Its restart policy is the supervisor loop you wrote by hand in B2.17. Its resource limits are the cgroup files from B7, written by systemd on your behalf. Its logs are the stdout and stderr of a program that never learned what a log file is. Nothing here is new machinery; it is a management layer over machinery you have already built, and once you see that, the entire systemctl/journalctl surface stops being a set of incantations.

    We go in three movements. Units: what init is for, the unit types you actually meet, writing one from scratch, the keys that decide behaviour, and the ordering-versus-requirement distinction behind most "works on reboot, fails at boot" bugs. Operating: the verbs that matter, overriding without editing, keeping configuration out of the unit, and reading a failure from systemctl status alone. Logs and boot: journalctl fluency, making sure your logs survive the crash you need them for, and the boot-target and cgroup-slice picture that turns out to be the whole layout of a Kubernetes node on one screen. By the end, systemctl status kubelet and journalctl -u kubelet — the two commands you will run on every broken node for the rest of your career — read as ordinary prose.

    Checkpoints14 Hours~4 PrerequisitesB2, B7
    • Say what an init system is for, why PID 1 is special, and why the orphan-reaping duty from B2 is the same duty a container's PID 1 usually fails at.
    • Recognise the unit types you will actually meet — .service, .socket, .timer, .target, .mount — and say what each one is for.
    • Write a working unit file from scratch with its three sections, and know which directory wins when the same unit name exists in two of them.
    • Choose Restart=, RestartSec= and Type= deliberately, and explain which Type= mistake makes systemd supervise a process that has already exited.
    • Distinguish ordering (After=) from requirement (Requires=/Wants=) and explain the boot race that comes from treating them as one setting.
    • Use the systemctl verbs fluently, and never again confuse start with enable or restart with reload.
    • Override a vendor unit with a drop-in instead of editing it, including the empty-assignment trick needed to replace ExecStart.
    • Keep configuration out of the unit with EnvironmentFile=, and explain why editing that file changes nothing until the service restarts.
    • Read a failure from systemctl status alone and tell an exit code from a signal — the fork in the road between "read the app's logs" and "look outside the app".
    • Use journalctl with intent: one unit, follow, since, priority, this boot versus the previous one.
    • Check whether the journal survives a reboot and make it persistent deliberately, before the crash you need it for.
    • Read the boot picture — the default target, what took the longest, and the slice/scope cgroup tree that places every process on the machine.
    • Promote a hand-written supervisor into a real unit with a restart policy, an environment file, journald logging and boot enablement, then schedule a job with a .timer.
    • Diagnose a unit that will not start and a Restart=always loop that hides a config error behind an endless restart.

    B2 is the real prerequisite: a unit's status line is nothing but a process, an exit code and a signal (B2.8) with a nicer font, and the restart policy you will configure here is the supervisor loop you already wrote by hand (B2.17) — this module's project promotes that very script. B7 matters because systemd is what puts every service into a cgroup: the slices and scopes you met in B7.12 are systemd's doing, and the resource keys you can set on a unit are the cgroup files from that module with friendlier names. Everything runs on the throwaway sandbox VM (B0.1), never on the cluster. Nothing here needs installing — systemctl, journalctl, systemd-analyze and systemd-cgls all ship with the system. You will be writing files under /etc/systemd/system/ and restarting services, so most commands need sudo; the labs create units named hello, supervisor and sysreport, and the last lesson tells you how to remove them all. Take a snapshot first (B0.9) if you would rather roll back than clean up.

    B11.1

    What an init system is for, and why PID 1 is special

    Why it matters

    A booted Linux machine is not a natural state; it is a state something worked to produce. The kernel finishes initialising hardware and then starts exactly one program, and from that moment everything else on the machine — every daemon, every login, every network interface being configured — exists because that one program, or something it started, made it exist. Knowing what that program is responsible for is what lets you answer questions like "why did this start before that?", "who restarted my service?" and, eventually, "why is the kubelet the only Kubernetes component that systemd knows about?"

    The concept

    The kernel starts one userspace process, gives it PID 1, and gets out of the way. That process is the init system, and it has three duties that nothing else can do:

    • Bring the machine up. Start the services that should run, in an order that works, and keep going until the system has reached a defined working state. "Working state" is a real, named thing here, not a feeling — B11.12 shows you the target that defines it.
    • Keep it up. Supervise: notice when something dies, decide whether that was expected, and restart it if not. This is the loop from B2.17, running for every service on the machine at once.
    • Adopt the orphans. When a process's parent dies before it does, the kernel reparents the child to PID 1 (B2.9). PID 1 therefore inherits strangers continuously, and must wait() on them so they don't accumulate as zombies. This is not optional politeness; it is a structural duty that comes with the PID.

    PID 1 is also special to the kernel in a way worth knowing: signals with a default action are not delivered to it unless it has installed a handler. Send SIGTERM to a normal process with no handler and it dies; send it to PID 1 with no handler and nothing happens at all. The kernel is protecting itself — a machine whose PID 1 dies panics — but the consequence bites elsewhere, as you will see in a moment.

    systemd is the init system on Ubuntu and on essentially every distribution a Kubernetes node runs. It took on a great deal beyond the three duties above, which is both why it is powerful and why it is argued about: it is simultaneously the supervisor, the logger (B11.10), the cgroup manager (B11.12), a socket activator, a timer daemon and a mount manager. For our purposes that consolidation is good news, because it means one vocabulary — the unit — describes all of it.

    Now the part that pays off later. A container also has a PID 1: the process you told it to run, which lands as PID 1 inside its own PID namespace (B6.5). That process inherits both duties above and usually implements neither. It does not reap orphans, so a container that spawns subprocesses can slowly fill with zombies; and it typically has no SIGTERM handler, so the kernel refuses to deliver the polite stop signal at all and the process only dies when the grace period expires and SIGKILL arrives. That is the entire explanation for why some containers take exactly ten seconds to stop, and why small init programs like tini exist.

    kernel boots→PID 1 = systemd→start units·supervise·reap orphans

    One process is started by the kernel; everything else on the machine descends from it.

    Lab
    1. Find out what is in charge of your machine, and confirm it really is process number one. 
      multipass shell sandbox
ps -p 1 -o pid,comm,args --no-headers
            1 systemd /sbin/init                ← PID 1 is systemd (/sbin/init is a symlink to it)
    2. See that everything descends from it. The process tree has exactly one root, and it is the process you just identified. 
      ps -eo pid,ppid,comm --no-headers | awk '$2==0 {print "kernel-parented:", $0}'
pstree -p 1 | head -6
      kernel-parented:  1  0 systemd        ← the only process whose parent is the kernel
systemd(1)─┬─cron(812)
           ├─dbus-daemon(789)
           ├─sshd(1024)───sshd(1301)───bash(1310)      ← your own shell, several generations down
           └─systemd-journal(312)
    3. Watch the adoption duty happen. Start a process whose parent exits immediately, then look at who its parent has become — you will find PID 1 has quietly taken responsibility for a process it never started. 
      bash -c 'nohup sleep 300 &' ; sleep 1
ps -eo pid,ppid,comm | grep '[s]leep 300\|[0-9] sleep'
         2451      1 sleep          ← PPID 1: the original parent exited, systemd adopted it
    4. Prove the PID-1 signal rule, safely, using a container-shaped experiment rather than the real PID 1. Run a shell as PID 1 inside its own PID namespace (B6.5) and try to SIGTERM it from outside. 
      sudo unshare --pid --fork --mount-proc sleep 60 &
sleep 1; pid=$(pgrep -f 'sleep 60' | head -1)
sudo kill -TERM "$pid"; sleep 1
ps -p "$pid" -o pid,comm --no-headers && echo "still alive"
         2698 sleep
still alive        ← SIGTERM had no effect: no handler installed, and it is PID 1 in its namespace
    # sudo kill -KILL "$pid"   ← only SIGKILL, which cannot be ignored, ends it
    5. Clean up the leftovers from the experiments above. 
      sudo pkill -KILL -f 'sleep 60'; pkill -f 'sleep 300'; echo cleaned
      cleaned
    Verify

    Answer three things without looking. What starts PID 1, and what happens to the machine if PID 1 exits? (The kernel starts it; if it exits the kernel panics, which is why the kernel protects it from unhandled default-action signals.) Why must PID 1 reap orphans? (Because the kernel reparents every orphan to it, so it accumulates children it never started and must wait() on them or leak zombies.) And why does a container process that ignores SIGTERM take the full grace period to stop? (It is PID 1 in its own PID namespace with no handler installed, so the kernel does not deliver the signal at all; only the later SIGKILL ends it.)

    Gotchas

    The PID-1 signal rule catches people precisely because it is invisible when it works and baffling when it does not. A program that handles SIGTERM perfectly well as an ordinary process becomes unstoppable-by-TERM the moment it is PID 1 without a handler, because the kernel silently declines to deliver the signal rather than reporting an error. If a process is ignoring a signal you are certain you sent, check whether it is PID 1 in some namespace before you doubt your kill command.

    The second trap is treating "init system" as a synonym for systemd. It is one implementation of the role; others exist (sysvinit, OpenRC, runit, and inside containers, tini or dumb-init). What is universal is the role — PID 1, adoption, supervision — and that is what transfers when you meet a system that does not run systemd. Learn the role first and the specific tool second.

    On a Kubernetes node, systemd runs exactly one Kubernetes component: the kubelet. Everything else — the API server, the scheduler, the controller manager, etcd on a control-plane node — runs as a static pod, started by the kubelet from YAML files on disk rather than by systemd. That arrangement exists because of a chicken-and-egg problem: the API server cannot be scheduled by the machinery that only exists once the API server is running. So the boot order on a control-plane node is systemd → kubelet → everything else, which is why "restart the API server" means moving a file, not running systemctl restart. This single fact reshapes how you troubleshoot a node, and it is the reason this module exists.

    How each component actually runs on the node, static pods and mirror pods included, is W1.2; the kubelet's own supervision loop and its three pod sources is W1.19.

    Docs

    man 1 systemd is the top-level page and describes the init role and PID 1's special treatment; man 1 init redirects there on this system. man 7 signal for the rule that default-action signals are not delivered to PID 1 without a handler. man 5 systemd.unit is the page you will return to for the rest of this module. For the container side, man 7 pid_namespaces explains why every container has a PID 1 of its own.

    B11.2

    The unit types you will actually meet

    Why it matters

    systemd manages eleven kinds of thing, which sounds like a lot until you notice that five of them cover almost everything you will ever touch. Knowing which is which saves you from the two most common confusions: looking for a service that is actually a timer, and wondering why multi-user.target has no process attached to it. It also explains why a filesystem, a scheduled job and a daemon are managed with the same three commands.

    The concept

    A unit is systemd's word for "a thing I manage." The file's extension declares its type, and the type determines what the file may contain. The five that matter:

    • .service — a process to run and supervise. This is the overwhelming majority of what you will write and read: a daemon, a one-shot script, a database, the kubelet.
    • .socket — a listening socket that systemd holds open on a service's behalf, starting the service on the first connection. This is socket activation: the port is bound at boot, but the program only starts when someone actually connects, and connections that arrive during start-up wait rather than being refused.
    • .timer — a schedule that starts another unit. This is systemd's replacement for cron, and its advantages are concrete: the job's output goes to the journal automatically, a missed run can be caught up after downtime, and the job is a unit like any other, so it gets status, logs and resource limits for free.
    • .target — a named group of units, with no process of its own. A target is a synchronisation point and a goal: multi-user.target means "the set of units that constitutes a working multi-user system." Targets replaced the old numbered runlevels with something you can actually read.
    • .mount — a filesystem mount, managed as a unit. Every line in /etc/fstab (B5.4) is silently turned into one of these at boot, which is why a failed mount shows up in systemctl at all.

    The remaining types exist and are worth recognising rather than learning: .path (act when a file appears or changes), .slice (a cgroup branch for resource management — B11.12), .scope (a cgroup for processes systemd did not start, like a login session), .device, .swap and .automount.

    Two naming conventions will otherwise confuse you. A templated unit has an @ in its name: getty@tty1.service is an instance of the template getty@.service, with tty1 passed in as the instance name — one file, many instances. And a .timer by default starts the .service with the same base name, which is why timers and services come in pairs: sysreport.timer starts sysreport.service unless told otherwise.

    Lab

    Name the five types and what each manages, then answer two questions from them. Why does multi-user.target have no process? (A target is a named group of units and a synchronisation point, not something to run.) And if you find a backup.timer on a machine, what other unit almost certainly exists and why? (backup.service — a timer starts the service with the same base name by default, so timers come in pairs.)

    Gotchas

    The commonest confusion is looking in the wrong place for a scheduled job. If someone says "the nightly backup runs at 2am," systemctl status backup.service will usually show it as inactive — which is correct and not a fault, because the service only runs when the timer starts it. Check systemctl list-timers for when it last ran and when it will run next; the service being idle between runs is the design.

    The second is assuming a socket unit's service must be running for the port to be open. With socket activation the socket unit holds the port while the service is stopped, so ss -ltnp shows something listening and systemctl status shows the service inactive, and both are telling the truth. This is exactly how systemd-journald and, on many systems, sshd are arranged.

    On a Kubernetes node you will meet only two of these types in anger, but you will meet them constantly: kubelet.service and containerd.service, both ordinary .service units. Everything else Kubernetes-shaped on that node is not a systemd unit at all — pods are managed by the kubelet, and the containers inside them are managed by containerd, neither of which registers anything with systemd. The one place the other types show up is the cgroup tree: kubepods.slice is a .slice unit, which is how a node's pod resources are carved out of the machine's (B11.12 and B7.12).

    What actually runs as a systemd unit on a node versus as a static pod is W1.2; the kubelet's relationship with containerd over CRI is W1.20.

    B11.3

    Write a unit file from scratch — three sections, nothing more

    Why it matters

    A unit file looks intimidating in the wild because real ones are long. Written from scratch it is about six lines, and writing those six lines yourself is what converts systemctl from a set of memorised commands into something you understand. It is also a genuinely useful skill: turning "this script needs to keep running" into a supervised, logged, boot-surviving service is a ten-minute job once you have done it once.

    The concept

    A .service file has three sections, and each answers one question:

    • [Unit] — what is this and how does it relate to other units? A Description= (which is what systemctl status prints), plus the ordering and dependency keys covered in B11.5.
    • [Service] — what do I run and how do I supervise it? ExecStart= and the behaviour keys covered in B11.4. This section only exists for .service units.
    • [Install] — what should happen when someone enables this? Almost always a single WantedBy=multi-user.target, meaning "when the machine boots to a working multi-user state, want me too." If you omit this section the unit can still be started by hand but cannot be enabled, which is a confusing failure the first time it happens.

    Where you put the file decides who wins. systemd reads unit files from several directories in a fixed precedence order, and a file in a higher-priority directory completely replaces the same-named file in a lower one:

    • /etc/systemd/system/ — yours, the administrator's. Highest priority. This is where you write new units and where package upgrades never touch.
    • /run/systemd/system/ — runtime, generated, lives in memory, vanishes at reboot.
    • /usr/lib/systemd/system/ — the vendor's, installed by packages. Lowest priority. On Ubuntu 24.04 /lib is a symlink to /usr, so /lib/systemd/system/ is the same directory under an older name.

    That ordering is the whole reason for the classic mistake: editing a unit in /usr/lib/systemd/system/ works perfectly until the next apt upgrade replaces the file and silently reverts your change. Write to /etc/systemd/system/, or better, use a drop-in (B11.7) so you override one key instead of owning the whole file.

    One rule catches everyone once: after creating or editing a unit file, systemd does not notice by itself. You must run systemctl daemon-reload to make it re-read the files from disk. Forget it and you will restart a service repeatedly, watch nothing change, and doubt your own file.

    write /etc/systemd/system/hello.service→systemctl daemon-reload→systemctl start hello→systemctl status hello

    The four-step cycle you will repeat for every unit you ever write; skipping step two is the classic first mistake.

    Lab
    1. Write something for the unit to run. A script that prints and sleeps is enough to be supervised, and printing to stdout means you get logging for free (B11.10). 
      multipass shell sandbox
cat > /home/ubuntu/hello.sh <<'EOF'
#!/usr/bin/env bash
while true; do
  echo "hello from pid $$ at $(date -Is)"
  sleep 5
done
EOF
chmod +x /home/ubuntu/hello.sh
                             # no output; the script is ready
    2. Write the unit itself — three sections, six meaningful lines. Note that ExecStart takes an absolute path: systemd does not run a shell for you, so there is no PATH lookup and no ~ expansion. 
      sudo tee /etc/systemd/system/hello.service >/dev/null <<'EOF'
[Unit]
Description=A hello service, written by hand

[Service]
ExecStart=/home/ubuntu/hello.sh

[Install]
WantedBy=multi-user.target
EOF
sudo systemctl daemon-reload
                             # daemon-reload is silent when it succeeds
    3. Start it and read its status. This output is the one you will read thousands of times — take a moment over each line. 
      sudo systemctl start hello
systemctl status hello --no-pager
      ● hello.service - A hello service, written by hand        ← your Description=
     Loaded: loaded (/etc/systemd/system/hello.service; disabled; preset: enabled)
     Active: active (running) since Sat 2026-07-25 09:14:02 UTC; 3s ago
   Main PID: 4412 (hello.sh)
      Tasks: 2 (limit: 4613)
     CGroup: /system.slice/hello.service           ← B7's cgroup, created for you
             ├─4412 /bin/bash /home/ubuntu/hello.sh
             └─4429 sleep 5

    4. Notice what the status said: disabled. The service is running now but would not come back after a reboot. Enable it and watch what enabling actually does — it is one symlink, driven by your [Install] section. 
      sudo systemctl enable hello
ls -l /etc/systemd/system/multi-user.target.wants/hello.service
      Created symlink /etc/systemd/system/multi-user.target.wants/hello.service → /etc/systemd/system/hello.service.
lrwxrwxrwx 1 root root 36 ... hello.service -> /etc/systemd/system/hello.service
    ← "enabled" is literally this symlink: multi-user.target now wants your unit
    5. Prove the precedence rule rather than believing it. Ask systemd which file it actually loaded, and confirm the vendor directory is a different place. 
      systemctl show hello -p FragmentPath
ls /usr/lib/systemd/system/ | head -3
      FragmentPath=/etc/systemd/system/hello.service     ← yours, the highest-priority location
apt-daily.service                                     ← the vendor directory, full of package units
console-getty.service
cron.service
    Verify

    systemctl status hello should show active (running), and after the enable the Loaded: line should say enabled rather than disabled. You should be able to state what each of the three sections is for in one phrase each, what enable physically created (a symlink in multi-user.target.wants/), and what happens if you edit a unit file without running daemon-reload (nothing — systemd keeps using the version it read last). Leave hello.service running; the next few lessons modify it.

    Gotchas

    Forgetting daemon-reload is the mistake everyone makes, and its symptom is uniquely misleading: the restart succeeds, systemd reports no error, and the old behaviour continues. If a change to a unit file appears to have no effect, run daemon-reload before investigating anything else. (systemd will usually warn you with "Warning: The unit file … changed on disk" on the next systemctl command, but only after you have already been confused once.)

    The second is expecting a shell. ExecStart= is not a shell command line: there is no PATH search (use an absolute path), no ~ expansion, no pipes, no redirection, no globbing and no &&. If you genuinely need shell features, be explicit about it — ExecStart=/bin/sh -c 'foo | bar > /tmp/out' — rather than writing something that looks like shell and silently is not. A unit whose ExecStart contains a pipe character will start a program with a literal | as an argument.

    Third, do not edit files in /usr/lib/systemd/system/. It works, which is what makes it dangerous — the next package upgrade overwrites your change with no warning and the service quietly reverts to vendor behaviour, often weeks later when nobody connects the two events. Put your own units in /etc/systemd/system/ and override vendor units with drop-ins (B11.7).

    kubelet.service is a unit file exactly like the one you just wrote — a [Unit] with a description and ordering, a [Service] with an ExecStart pointing at /usr/bin/kubelet, and an [Install] with WantedBy=multi-user.target. When you eventually run systemctl cat kubelet on a node, you will be reading the same three sections, with the interesting part being how kubeadm adds its flags — through a drop-in rather than by editing the file, for exactly the precedence reason above.

    Reading how the node's components are actually launched, kubelet unit included, is W1.2; the kubelet's own directory of state and configuration is W1.23.

    Docs

    man 5 systemd.unit covers the [Unit] and [Install] sections and — importantly — the "Unit File Load Path" section that lists the precedence order above. man 5 systemd.service covers [Service]. man 5 systemd.syntax explains the file format itself (sections, comments, line continuation). man 1 systemctl for daemon-reload, enable, show -p and cat.

    B11.4

    The [Service] keys that decide behaviour — Restart, RestartSec and Type

    Why it matters

    Your unit currently runs a program. These keys turn it into a supervised program: one that comes back when it dies, waits sensibly before retrying, runs as the right user, and — the one that produces genuinely baffling failures — is correctly understood by systemd to be "up." Type= in particular is the key that, set wrong, makes systemd supervise a process that has already exited and restart the wrong thing forever.

    The concept

    The keys worth knowing, in rough order of how often they matter:

    • ExecStart= — the command, absolute path, no shell (B11.3). Siblings: ExecStartPre= and ExecStartPost= run before and after, and ExecStop=/ExecReload= define what stop and reload mean if the defaults won't do.
    • Restart= — when to bring it back. no (the default — nothing is restarted unless you ask for it), on-failure (a non-zero exit, a signal, a timeout — the sane choice for most services), always (even a clean exit 0), plus the narrower on-abnormal, on-abort and on-watchdog. This is the decision your B2.17 supervisor made in its own code; here it is one word.
    • RestartSec= — how long to wait before restarting. The default is 100ms, which is fast enough that a program crashing on start-up will spin. Set it to something human (2s, 5s) whenever a restart involves reconnecting to anything.
    • User= / Group= — run as somebody other than root. Free, and the single cheapest security improvement available to a unit; this is exactly what system users exist for (B4.3).
    • WorkingDirectory= — the directory the process starts in. Without it you inherit /, which is why relative paths inside a script behave differently under systemd than in your shell.
    • Type= — how systemd decides the service is up. This one deserves its own paragraph.

    Type= answers "when may I consider start-up finished and move on to units ordered after me?" The values you will meet:

    • simple (the default) — the process systemd started is the service; consider it up immediately.
    • exec — like simple, but wait until the exec() has actually succeeded, so a typo'd binary path is reported as a start failure rather than a silent later death. A better default than simple when you have the choice.
    • forking — the program forks a background child and the original exits; treat the child as the service. Old-style daemons do this. Usually paired with PIDFile= so systemd knows which process to watch.
    • oneshot — the process is expected to run and exit; the service is "up" once it has finished successfully. Right for scripts that do a job rather than stay resident, and the type a .timer's service almost always uses. Pair with RemainAfterExit=yes if "done" should count as "active."
    • notify — the program tells systemd when it is ready, over a socket. The most accurate option, and only available if the program was written to support it.

    The failure mode to remember: declare Type=simple for a daemon that forks, and systemd will supervise the original process, which exits immediately after forking. systemd sees the service die a fraction of a second after start, restarts it per your policy, and the real daemon accumulates in the background, unmanaged, one copy per restart. The symptom — "systemd keeps restarting it but it is clearly running" — is unmistakable once you have seen it, and the fix is Type=forking.

    One more key belongs here because it saves the drill in B11.14 from being infinite: systemd rate-limits restarts. StartLimitIntervalSec= (default 10s) and StartLimitBurst= (default 5) live in [Unit], and if a unit starts more than burst times within the interval, systemd gives up and puts it in the failed state with the message "start request repeated too quickly." That message means the restart loop was stopped by the rate limiter, not that the last start failed differently — a distinction people misread constantly.

    Lab
    1. Give your unit a restart policy and a user, then reload. Note that changing a unit file needs daemon-reload and a restart for the running instance to pick it up. 
      sudo tee /etc/systemd/system/hello.service >/dev/null <<'EOF'
[Unit]
Description=A hello service, written by hand

[Service]
Type=exec
ExecStart=/home/ubuntu/hello.sh
Restart=always
RestartSec=2s
User=ubuntu
WorkingDirectory=/home/ubuntu

[Install]
WantedBy=multi-user.target
EOF
sudo systemctl daemon-reload && sudo systemctl restart hello
systemctl show hello -p Restart -p RestartUSec -p User -p Type
      Restart=always
RestartUSec=2s
User=ubuntu
Type=exec
    2. Watch the supervision actually work. Kill the process the way an outsider would, and watch systemd bring it back two seconds later with a new PID — the loop from B2.17, now written as one word of configuration. 
      pid=$(systemctl show hello -p MainPID --value); echo "before: $pid"
sudo kill -KILL "$pid"; sleep 3
echo "after:  $(systemctl show hello -p MainPID --value)"
systemctl show hello -p NRestarts
      before: 4412
after:  4501            ← a different PID: it was restarted, not resurrected
NRestarts=1              ← systemd counts restarts for you; remember this for B11.14
    3. See the difference Restart= makes by asking for a clean exit. Stop the service properly and confirm systemd does not fight you — an operator-requested stop is never restarted, whatever the policy says. 
      sudo systemctl stop hello; sleep 3
systemctl is-active hello; echo "---"; sudo systemctl start hello
      inactive        ← Restart=always does not mean "un-stoppable"; a deliberate stop wins
    4. Reproduce the rate limiter, because you will meet its message on a real machine. Point a unit at a program that fails instantly and watch systemd give up after five tries. 
      sudo tee /etc/systemd/system/flap.service >/dev/null <<'EOF'
[Unit]
Description=A service that fails immediately

[Service]
ExecStart=/bin/false
Restart=always
RestartSec=100ms
EOF
sudo systemctl daemon-reload; sudo systemctl start flap; sleep 6
systemctl is-active flap; systemctl status flap --no-pager | tail -3
      failed
flap.service: Scheduled restart job, restart counter is at 5.
flap.service: Start request repeated too quickly.
flap.service: Failed with result 'exit-code'.     ← the rate limiter stopped the loop
    5. Clean up the flapping unit; hello stays for the next lessons. 
      sudo systemctl reset-failed flap; sudo rm /etc/systemd/system/flap.service
sudo systemctl daemon-reload; echo removed
      removed
    Verify

    You should have watched a killed process come back with a different PID, and a deliberately stopped service stay stopped. Explain in one sentence each: what Type= decides (when systemd considers start-up complete, and therefore which process it supervises), what goes wrong if you declare Type=simple for a forking daemon (systemd watches the parent, which exits immediately, so it restarts forever while the real daemon piles up unmanaged), and what "start request repeated too quickly" means (the restart rate limiter gave up — StartLimitBurst starts within StartLimitIntervalSec).

    Gotchas

    Restart=always is not the safe default it looks like. It hides failures: a service that cannot possibly work — bad config, missing file, wrong permissions — will restart forever, presenting as "active" for a fraction of every second and never producing an alert, while the journal fills with the same error at whatever rate the rate limiter allows. That is precisely the second fault in B11.14. Prefer Restart=on-failure, which restarts real crashes and respects a clean exit as a decision rather than a problem.

    The default RestartSec=100ms is too fast for almost anything real. A service that reconnects to a database or waits for the network will retry ten times a second, hammer whatever it depends on, and hit the start limiter within a second or two — turning a transient dependency problem into a hard failure. Set an explicit RestartSec whenever a restart does more than re-exec a trivial process.

    Finally, User= changes more than the uid. The process gets that user's privileges, and anything the unit touches — working directory, files it writes, sockets it binds — must be reachable by that user. A unit that works as root and fails with User=someone is almost always a permission problem on a path (B4.6 — remember that traversing a directory needs x on every component), and the journal will say so plainly if you look.

    Restart= is the node-level ancestor of a pod's restartPolicy, and the resemblance is not superficial: Always, OnFailure and Never mean here what they mean there, and both use exponential backoff so a crash loop cannot become a busy loop. When you later meet CrashLoopBackOff, you are looking at exactly this mechanism one layer up — a supervisor restarting a failing process with a growing delay — and the debugging move is the same one you just did: get the exit status, then read the logs of the previous attempt rather than the current one. systemd's NRestarts counter is the direct analogue of a pod's restart count.

    Container states and the three status vocabularies are W3.1; CrashLoopBackOff, --previous and the exit-code alphabet are W6.16.

    Docs

    man 5 systemd.service is the page — read the Type= and Restart= entries in full, they are short and they are the entire content of this lesson. man 5 systemd.exec covers User=, WorkingDirectory=, environment handling and the sandboxing keys you will meet in B12. man 5 systemd.unit for StartLimitIntervalSec= and StartLimitBurst= (they live in [Unit], not [Service] — a common source of "why is my setting ignored").

    B11.5

    Ordering is not requirement: After, Requires and Wants

    Why it matters

    These three keys look like variations on one idea and are in fact two completely independent ideas that happen to be written next to each other. Merging them is the direct cause of nearly every "it works when I restart it by hand, but fails at boot" bug — the kind that reproduces only on a cold machine, at the worst possible time, and vanishes the moment you try to investigate it interactively.

    The concept

    systemd separates two questions that most people ask as one:

    • Requirement — must that other unit be running? Answered by Requires= (yes: if it fails or is stopped, I am stopped too) and Wants= (preferably: try to start it, but carry on regardless if it fails). This is about dependency.
    • Ordering — in what sequence? Answered by After= ("do not start me before that unit has finished starting") and Before= (the mirror image). This is about time.

    Crucially, neither implies the other. A unit with Requires=postgresql.service and no After= is telling systemd "start Postgres too" — and systemd, being a parallel starter, will happily start both at the same instant. Your service then races Postgres and loses roughly whenever the machine is cold and slow, which is to say at boot. Add After=postgresql.service and the race disappears. The pairing you almost always want is both:

    [Unit]
Wants=postgresql.service
After=postgresql.service

    Wants= + After= is the workhorse combination: pull the dependency in, and wait for it, but do not make my service collapse if it has a bad day. Reserve Requires= for genuine "there is no point in me existing without it" cases, and know its sharp edge — Requires= propagates stops as well as failures, so stopping the dependency stops you too, which is occasionally a nasty surprise during maintenance.

    Two more mechanisms complete the picture. BindsTo= is Requires= with teeth: it also stops you if the other unit stops for any reason, including the device behind it disappearing. And targets act as ordering landmarks — After=network-online.target is how you say "once the network is genuinely usable," which is subtly different from network.target (meaning "networking has been configured," which can be true before any address is reachable). If a service needs to make an outbound connection at start-up, network-online.target is the one you want, and it needs a corresponding Wants= to actually be pulled in.

    Wants= (pull it in)+After= (wait for it)=the combination that survives a cold boot

    Dependency and sequence are separate switches; almost every boot race is one of them being set without the other.

    Lab
    1. Look at a real unit's relationships. ssh.service is a good specimen because it expresses both kinds of key. 
      systemctl show ssh -p Wants -p Requires -p After -p Before | tr ' ' '\n' | grep -v '^$' | head -12
      Wants=network.target
After=network.target
After=auditd.service
Requires=sshd-keygen.target        ← a genuine "cannot work without" dependency
    ← note the same unit appearing under both Wants= and After=: pull in AND wait
    2. Create the race deliberately, so you can see that requirement alone does not order anything. Make a slow unit and a second unit that requires it without waiting for it. 
      sudo tee /etc/systemd/system/slow.service >/dev/null <<'EOF'
[Unit]
Description=A dependency that takes its time
[Service]
Type=oneshot
RemainAfterExit=yes
ExecStart=/bin/sh -c 'sleep 5; echo slow is ready'
EOF
sudo tee /etc/systemd/system/racer.service >/dev/null <<'EOF'
[Unit]
Description=Requires slow, but does not wait for it
Requires=slow.service
[Service]
Type=oneshot
ExecStart=/bin/sh -c 'echo racer ran at $(date +%T); systemctl is-active slow.service'
EOF
sudo systemctl daemon-reload; sudo systemctl start racer
                             # returns quickly — suspiciously quickly
    3. Read the journal and see the race in the timestamps: the racer ran and finished while its stated requirement was still starting. 
      journalctl -u racer -u slow --since '2 min ago' --no-pager -o short-precise | tail -6

09:31:02.121 sandbox sh[5013]: activating            ← slow.service was still starting
09:31:07.140 sandbox sh[5001]: slow is ready             ← five seconds later
    ← Requires= pulled slow in; nothing told systemd to wait for it

      sudo mkdir -p /etc/systemd/system/racer.service.d
printf '[Unit]\nAfter=slow.service\n' | sudo tee /etc/systemd/system/racer.service.d/10-order.conf

09:33:41.889 sandbox sh[5121]: active          ← now slow.service was finished first

      sudo rm -rf /etc/systemd/system/racer.service /etc/systemd/system/racer.service.d /etc/systemd/system/slow.service

    Verify

    State the two independent questions and which keys answer each (requirement: Requires=/Wants=; ordering: After=/Before=). Then explain why a unit with Requires= and no After= can start before the thing it requires (systemd starts units in parallel; requirement pulls the other unit in but says nothing about sequence), and give the combination you should reach for by default (Wants= plus After= on the same unit). You should have watched exactly that failure and its one-line fix in the journal timestamps.

    Gotchas

    The reason this bug is so expensive is that it does not reproduce interactively. When you restart the service by hand, its dependency has been running for hours, so the race is won trivially every time and everything looks correct. It only fails on a cold boot, when both units start together and the slow one loses — which means the failure appears at reboot, in production, usually at 3am, and cannot be reproduced by the person investigating. If a service works on restart but fails on boot, look at After= before anything else.

    Requires= propagating stops is the second surprise. Stop the dependency for maintenance and everything that Requires= it stops too, immediately and without asking. That is correct behaviour and rarely what the person typing systemctl stop expected. Use Wants= unless you truly mean "I cannot exist without this," and know that BindsTo= is stricter still.

    Finally, network-online.target is not free: it is only reached if something pulls it in, so After=network-online.target alone can order you after a target that never activates. The idiom is Wants=network-online.target together with After=network-online.target — the same pairing as everything else in this lesson, which is a good sign you have understood the rule.

    The kubelet unit is the working example of this pairing on a node: it needs the container runtime to be reachable before it can do anything useful, and getting the ordering right is the difference between a node that comes up cleanly and one that logs runtime-connection errors for the first minute after every reboot. Those errors are harmless-but-alarming and turn up in real incident reviews constantly — now you know they are an ordering artefact rather than a fault. Kubernetes has no equivalent of these keys for pods, incidentally: pod start-up ordering is expressed with init containers and readiness probes instead, which is a deliberate design choice worth noticing.

    How the kubelet and containerd relate on a node is W1.20; the NotReady ladder you will walk when that relationship breaks is W6.10.
