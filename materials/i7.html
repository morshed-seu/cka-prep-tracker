    I7 — Runtime architecture: containerd, shims, snapshotters

    The heaviest module on the track — plan two sittings. Everything above runc and below the kubelet, assembled: who runs, who stays running, what survives a daemon restart, and where each of the three on-disk stores physically is.

    Every module so far handed you one piece. I2 gave you the work order, I3 the contractor who reads it, I4 the flat-pack kit, I5 the warehouse it ships from, I6 the factory that packs it. containerd is the building-services company that employs all of them — and this is the module where the pieces stop being separate. By the end, nothing on a node's container layer is opaque to you, including every object crictl and kubectl politely hide.

    The process tree

    I7.1

    Where did runc go, and who is the workload's parent?

    Why it matters

    Everyone who runs containers eventually draws the process tree on a whiteboard, and almost everyone draws it wrong. The wrong picture — containerd as the parent of everything, with runc sitting alongside the workload babysitting it — makes two very common node incidents unexplainable: why restarting containerd does not kill your pods, and why ps on a busy node never shows a single runc. Draw the tree once from a live machine and both stop being mysteries.

    The concept

    In I3.5 you watched runc create return to your prompt while the container stayed up, and you learned why: runc is a contractor. It arrives with the work order, builds the namespaces, pivots the root, hands the job to the workload, and leaves. Nothing about that changes when containerd is driving instead of you. So the question I3 planted — if runc leaves, who holds the door open? — has to have an answer, and the answer is a fourth process that most diagrams omit entirely.

    That process is the shim, containerd-shim-runc-v2. It is the on-site foreman: the contractor finishes and drives away, but somebody has to stay in the building, keep the phone line open and sign for the work. Concretely, the shim holds three things nobody else can:

    • The workload's stdio. Something must be on the other end of the container's stdout pipe, or of its pty for an interactive container. If containerd held that end, stopping containerd would close the pipe under a running workload.
    • The exit status. On Linux only a process's parent can reap it and read its exit code. Whoever is to report "container exited 137" must be the parent — and must still be alive when it happens.
    • The identity of the container while containerd is not running at all.

    Now the part that surprises people. The shim is the workload's parent, but the shim's own parent is PID 1 — systemd — not containerd. containerd starts the shim detached, so it is immediately re-parented to init. That is deliberate, and it is the whole trick: because containerd is never an ancestor of anything that matters, containerd can die, be upgraded and come back without a single workload noticing. You will prove exactly that in I7.3.

    So the lineage of who started whom is containerd → shim → workload, but the live ancestry you see in ps is systemd → shim → workload. Both pictures are true; they answer different questions, and conflating them is what produces the wrong whiteboard drawing.

    containerdstarts, detachedshimfork/execruncexitsworkload

    Read that as a timeline, not as a family tree: only the shim and the workload are still alive by the time you type ps.

    containerd's own runtime-v2 documentation is explicit that containerd does not run the container itself — it talks to something that outlives the call:

    However, containerd itself does not actually directly invoke the runtime to start the container. Instead it expects to invoke the runtime, which will expose a socket - Unix-domain on Unix-like systems, named pipe on Windows - and listen for container commands via ttRPC over that socket.

    and that the shim, not containerd, is what calls runc:

    The shim then invokes the actual runc binary, passing it the container configuration, and the runc binary does the actual work of starting the container.

    Verify

    You can draw the tree from memory and defend every edge: the workload's parent is a shim; the shim's parent is init; containerd is an ancestor of nothing; and runc is a process that existed for a few milliseconds at creation time and has not existed since.

    Gotchas
    • ctr run -d --rm is rejected outright — ctr: flags --detach and --rm cannot be specified together. --rm means "delete the container when the task exits", which requires ctr to still be attached and watching. Detach and you own the cleanup.
    • "The shim is containerd's child" is the single most common wrong belief here, and it survives because the lineage diagram in every blog post looks like a process tree. Check PPID on a real machine before you argue about it.
    • A missing runc in ps is not evidence that something is wrong. On a node with a hundred pods, seeing zero runc processes is the healthy state. Seeing several stuck ones is the interesting case — that is a container wedged mid-creation, not a container running.
    • --forest will not rescue the wrong mental model. ps -ef --forest shows the same re-parented layout; it just makes it easier to misread, because the shim appears at top level next to other system daemons rather than under containerd.

    This is the tree behind W1.20 — kubelet → containerd → shim → runc, and it is why that arrow diagram must be read as a call chain rather than a ps hierarchy. On a real node the kubelet is a child of systemd, containerd is a child of systemd, and every shim is also a child of systemd — three independent trees, coupled only by sockets. When W6.13 asks you to narrate a node's death timeline, this is the structure that decides what dies in what order.

    Docs: search containerd's docs for runtime-v2 (the shim contract and its flow diagram), and ps(1) for --forest and the ppid output column. On the Kubernetes side, "container runtime interface" is the page that draws the same chain one layer up.

    I7.2

    The shim's directory, which is also the bundle

    Why it matters

    The shim is not just a process — it is a process with a directory, and that directory is where a stuck container's evidence lives. When ctr hangs, when a pod will not terminate, when you need to know what a running container was actually configured with rather than what its manifest claims, this is where you look. It is also the moment the whole track joins up: the shim's working directory turns out to be the OCI bundle you hand-wrote in I2.

    The concept

    Every task gets a directory under /run/containerd/io.containerd.runtime.v2.task/<namespace>/<id>/. The path encodes three things you now know how to read: io.containerd.runtime.v2 is the plugin that owns it (I7.8), the namespace is containerd's own tenancy boundary (I7.5), and the id is the container's name.

    Because it lives under /run, which is a tmpfs, the whole thing evaporates on reboot — correctly, since none of it describes anything that survives a reboot anyway.

    What is inside:

    FileWhat it is for
    config.jsonThe OCI runtime spec — literally the document you wrote by hand in I2.2, generated for you from the image config. ociVersion 1.3.0, the same one runc --version claims.
    rootfs/The mounted root filesystem — the snapshotter's overlay stack, mounted here (I7.11).
    init.pidThe workload's PID as plain text, so state can be rebuilt from disk after containerd restarts.
    bootstrap.jsonThe shim's ttrpc socket address — how containerd finds this shim again.
    logA named pipe the shim writes to and containerd drains into its journal.
    shim-binary-pathWhich shim binary owns this task, so a later delete uses the same one.
    options.jsonRuntime options passed in CreateTaskRequest — {} when you accept every default.
    workA symlink to the task's persistent state under /var/lib/containerd/.

    Two things here have changed and the internet has not caught up. Older guides tell you to cat a file called address to find the shim's socket. On containerd 2.3.3 there is no address file — that information moved into bootstrap.json, which also records the protocol version and names ttrpc explicitly. And the socket itself is no longer an abstract-namespace address: it is a real path under /run/containerd/s/, named by a hash, reached through the s symlink in the task directory.

    On the "one shim per container" question, be careful — the popular phrasing is a simplification of something the shim gets to decide for itself. With ctr, one container does get one shim. Under Kubernetes it is one shim per pod, with the pod's containers grouped underneath, which is why ps on a node shows far fewer shims than containers.

    The grouping is not containerd's rule at all — the documentation hands the decision to the shim:

    containerd does not know or care about whether the shim to container relationship is one-to-one, or one-to-many. It is entirely up to the shim to decide.

    and then names the Kubernetes case exactly:

    In practice, this means that containers launched by Kubernetes, that are part of the same Kubernetes pod, are handled by a single shim, grouping on the io.kubernetes.cri.sandbox-id label set by the CRI plugin.

    Gotchas
    • cat or tail on log hangs your terminal. It is a FIFO whose reader is containerd; opening it for reading as a second consumer just blocks. The shim's log output is already in the journal — use journalctl -u containerd instead. This one costs people a full session timeout the first time.

    • "One shim per container" is only true outside Kubernetes. If you count shims on a node and compare against container count, expect them to differ by design — that is pod grouping, not a leak.
    • Everything here is on tmpfs. Do not plan to post-mortem a crashed node by reading /run/containerd after a reboot; it is gone. The durable half is the work symlink's target under /var/lib/containerd.

    The pod-grouping rule above is the mechanism behind W1.21 — the pause container: the sandbox is what the shim is grouped around, and the pause process is what holds the pod's namespaces open while individual containers come and go. When W6.2 sends you hunting for logs below kubectl logs, the shim's FIFO into the containerd journal is the rung you are stepping onto.

    I7.3

    Restart the daemon, keep the containers

    Why it matters

    This is the single most reassuring fact about a Kubernetes node, and the one operators most often refuse to believe until they have watched it: you can restart containerd under running workloads and nothing dies. Not "usually survives", not "restarts quickly" — the workload processes are not signalled at all. Upgrading a container runtime on a production node is a routine operation because of this, and knowing it turns a scary maintenance window into a boring one.

    The concept

    You already have every piece of the explanation from I7.1 and I7.2; this checkpoint just makes you assemble it.

    containerd is not in the workload's ancestry, so killing it orphans nothing. The shim is the parent, the shim is a child of init, and the shim keeps holding the stdio, the pty and the right to reap. Meanwhile everything containerd needs in order to recognise the container again is on disk rather than in memory: the metadata store has the container record (I7.12), the task directory has init.pid, and bootstrap.json has the socket to dial. So a fresh containerd walks the task directories, reconnects to each shim over ttrpc, and rebuilds its view of the world from state it did not create.

    This is the same idea as the reconciliation loop from B13.12, applied to a daemon's own memory: containerd does not remember what is running, it re-derives it. Anything that can be re-derived can be lost safely.

    The three-sentence version for a colleague, which the checkpoint asks you to practise:

    Containerd isn't the parent of your container — a shim process is, and the shim's parent is init. Containerd only talks to that shim over a socket, and it writes down where the socket is, so when it comes back it just reconnects. Nothing ever signalled your process, so there was nothing for it to survive.

    The shim's exit-and-listen dance in the documented flow is exactly what makes this possible — the process that returns the socket address is not the process that keeps it:

    if new, the shim:
    1. creates a new process to listen on a socket for ttRPC commands from containerd
    2. returns the address to that socket to containerd
    3. exits

    Gotchas
    • This does not extend to killing the shim. The shim is load-bearing in a way containerd is not; kill it and you get the orphan described in I7.18. "The runtime is restartable" is a statement about the daemon only.
    • Events are lost, not queued. If a container exits during the restart window, the exit is delivered when containerd reconnects — but anything watching ctr events (I7.15) simply misses the gap. Clients that assume an unbroken event stream will drift.
    • An upgrade that changes the shim binary does not upgrade running shims. Existing containers keep the old shim — shim-binary-path records which one — until they are recreated. A node can legitimately be running two shim versions at once after an upgrade.
    • Restarting the kubelet is a different question with a different answer, and so is restarting containerd on a node where the kubelet is watching. The kubelet may decide to re-sync pods for its own reasons; that is a Kubernetes-level behaviour layered on top of this Linux-level one.

    This is why runtime upgrades appear in node-maintenance runbooks without a drain step, and it is the fact that makes W6.11's kubelet fault-hunting tractable: when you restart containerd while chasing a fault, you are not adding a second incident. It also sharpens W1.20 — the kubelet's connection to containerd is a gRPC client to a restartable server, so "containerd is down" and "the containers are down" are genuinely independent conditions on a node.

    Docs: systemctl(1) for restart versus reload, and ps(1) for the lstart and etime columns — the two that make "it never restarted" provable rather than asserted.

    I7.4

    Asking the layer below containerd

    Why it matters

    Every layer in this stack keeps its own records, and when they disagree you need to be able to ask each one separately. Being able to query runc directly — underneath containerd, underneath the shim — is what lets you tell "containerd has lost track of a container" apart from "the container is genuinely gone". That distinction is the whole of the orphan drill in I7.18.

    The concept

    In I3.9 you learned that runc keeps its state under a root directory, /run/runc by default, and that --root points it elsewhere. containerd uses that flag: each containerd namespace gets its own runc state directory under /run/containerd/runc/<namespace>. So on this box there is a default tree and a buildkit tree, because I6's builder was a separate tenant.

    The consequence is that you can run runc list against containerd's own state and see containerd's containers — with no daemon involved and no socket to dial. You are reading the same state.json you dissected in I3, written by the same runc, just in a directory containerd chose.

    And now the join that makes the module click. The BUNDLE column of that listing points at /run/containerd/io.containerd.runtime.v2.task/default/demo — the shim's directory from I7.2 is the runc bundle. They were never two things. containerd assembles a bundle in the place the shim will work from, and the shim hands that path to runc exactly as you handed a path to runc by hand in I3.

    ctrgRPCcontainerdttrpcshimargv + bundle pathrunc

    Four interfaces, three of them sockets, and the last one a directory on disk.

    The runtime spec defines the bundle as precisely this pairing of a config and a rootfs in one directory — which is why the shim's working directory can be the bundle without anything special being arranged:

    This bundle is the directory structure that is written to disk or transferred over the network. The bundle contains all the information needed to load and run a container.

    Gotchas

    • The namespace is part of the path. On a Kubernetes node the containers are under /run/containerd/runc/k8s.io, and --root /run/containerd/runc/default will again show you nothing at all. Same trap as ctr -n in I7.5, one layer down.
    • Do not write at this layer. runc delete or runc kill against containerd's root works, and leaves containerd holding a task record for something that no longer exists. Reading is diagnosis; writing is how you manufacture the fault in I7.18.
    • OWNER says root, not the container's user. It is who created the container, not who runs inside it — process.user.uid in config.json is the field you want for that.

    On an exam node this is the deepest rung of W6.2's fallback ladder: when kubectl is unavailable and crictl is also unhappy, runc --root /run/containerd/runc/k8s.io list still answers, because it reads files rather than talking to a daemon. It is also the concrete form of the bundle that W1.23 hints at when it walks you through /var/lib/kubelet.

    Docs: runc --help for --root, and the OCI runtime spec's bundle.md for the two-item definition of a bundle. Then runtime.md's state section for the fields runc state prints.

    I7.5

    The word that has two meanings, and the flag everyone forgets

    Why it matters

    This one checkpoint will save you an hour of confusion on a real node, and it is the single most common way people conclude that a Kubernetes node "has no images". You ssh in, run ctr images ls, get an empty table, and start diagnosing a problem that does not exist — because every pod's image is sitting in a different containerd namespace and ctr defaulted you into the empty one.

    The concept

    Containerd namespaces have nothing to do with Linux namespaces. Same word, unrelated idea, and the collision is unfortunate enough that it is worth saying out loud before anything else. A Linux namespace (B6.1) isolates a process's view of the kernel. A containerd namespace partitions the daemon's own bookkeeping — its containers, its image names, its snapshots — so that several clients can share one daemon without stepping on each other's names.

    The building-services analogy holds: containerd is one company serving the whole building, and a namespace is a client account. Two tenants can each have a container called web and nothing collides, because the account is part of the key.

    In practice you will meet three:

    • default — where ctr and nerdctl put things when you do not say otherwise.
    • k8s.io — where the CRI plugin puts everything the kubelet creates. On a node running Kubernetes, this is the only namespace you care about.
    • buildkit — I6's builder kept its own account, which is why I6.8's nerdctl run on a buildctl-built image decided the image was remote and tried to pull it.

    Now the subtle half, which matters more than the reflex. Namespaces separate names and metadata, not bytes. The content store on disk is one directory with no namespace anywhere in its path, and blobs in it are addressed by digest. So ctr -n fresh content ls genuinely shows zero blobs, while a pull into that namespace writes no new files at all — it only records that this account now references digests already present. You will measure exactly that below.

    That resolves an apparent contradiction from I5.15: a fresh containerd namespace is not a fresh node. If you are trying to reproduce a pull failure by switching namespaces, you are not clearing the cache — you are only clearing the index of it.

    Finally, and importantly: a namespace is not a security boundary. Any client that can reach the socket can name any namespace it likes.

    containerd's own documentation is unusually direct about both halves — the sharing and the non-security:

    Underlying image content is still shared via content addresses but image names and metadata are separate per namespace.
    It is important to note that namespaces, as implemented, is an administrative construct that is not meant to be used as a security feature. It is trivial for clients to switch namespaces.

    I7.6

    A record and a process are not the same object

    Why it matters

    "Container" is used for two different objects, and containerd is the layer where the difference becomes operationally real. Once you can say which of the two you are looking at, a whole class of confusing states stops being confusing: a container that exists but is not running, a stopped thing that still holds disk, and the delete that refuses with an error people find baffling.

    The concept

    In containerd:

    • A container is pure metadata — an OCI spec, an image reference, a snapshot key, some labels. It is a record in the database. Nothing is executing.
    • A task is a running process created from that record. It has a PID. It is the thing the shim is holding.

    The separation is not bureaucracy; it buys something specific. Because the container record already holds the resolved spec and the prepared snapshot, stopping and starting a workload does not re-resolve the image, re-check the registry or re-prepare the filesystem. The expensive work is done once at container-create time, and task start is cheap.

    If that sounds familiar, it should. It is exactly Kubernetes' own split between a container's definition in the pod spec and the instance that carries a restart count. A pod restarting in place is a new task against an unchanged container record, which is why the restart is fast and why the image is not pulled again.

    The state machine has one edge worth knowing precisely, because the tracker's phrasing of it turns out to be over-simplified. Killing a task does not remove it: the task goes to STOPPED and keeps its record and its old PID, so containerd can still be asked for its exit code. From there:

    • Deleting the container while its task is RUNNING fails: cannot delete a non stopped container.
    • Deleting the container while its task is STOPPED succeeds, and takes the task record with it — no separate ctr task rm is required.

    And the leak this sets up, which is the drill in I7.18: delete the task and forget the container, and the container record keeps referencing its snapshot forever. The process is gone, the disk is not. Nothing in ctr task ls will ever show it to you again.

    The distinction is visible in the API surface itself — containerd exposes them as separate gRPC services with separate lifecycles, containerd.services.containers.v1.Containers and containerd.services.tasks.v1.Tasks. The runtime spec draws the same line one layer down, where a container exists in the created state before anything runs:

    created : the runtime has finished the create operation (after the create operation is called), and the container process has neither exited nor executed the user-specified program
    OCI Runtime Specification v1.3.0 — runtime.md

    • The disk leak is silent by construction. ctr task ls cannot show you a container with no task, so the leaked snapshot is invisible to the command people check. ctr container ls and ctr snapshot ls are the ones that find it.
    • ctr run hides all of this by doing create + start + attach in one call, which is why the distinction stays invisible until the day it matters.

    This is the mechanism under W6.16's CrashLoopBackOff work: kubectl logs --previous and lastState.terminated.exitCode are readable precisely because the stopped task's record — and its exit code — outlive the process.     I7.7

    An image is a name pointing at one digest

    Why it matters

    Once you have seen that an image is just a name pointing at one digest, three otherwise-puzzling behaviours become obvious at a glance: why ctr images rm can free zero bytes, why two tags for the same image cost one copy, and why a node can be holding an image perfectly well while ctr images ls shows nothing you recognise. This is also the checkpoint where I4 and I5's work stops being an exercise and becomes the thing that is actually on disk.

    The concept

    Strip away the tooling and containerd's image model is one sentence: an image is a name with a target descriptor. The descriptor is a media type, a size and a digest. That is all the image record holds.

    Everything else is reachable by walking from that digest, exactly as pull.py walked it in I5.14: the digest fetches an index, the index names per-platform manifests, a manifest names a config and a list of layers. containerd stores all of those as undifferentiated blobs in the content store — it does not have an "image file" anywhere, because there is no such thing.

    Three consequences follow immediately, and they are the ones that catch people out:

    • Tagging is free. A second name pointing at the same digest adds one row and zero bytes.
    • ctr images rm deletes a name. The blobs stay until nothing references them and the garbage collector runs (I7.13). Deleting an image to free disk and watching df not move is the expected outcome, not a bug.
    • Content can be present without a recognisable name. Everything the kubelet pulls by digest is there by digest; blobs from a build or a half-finished pull have no name at all.

    The join to I4 is worth doing by hand once, and it is the lab below: take the digest out of ctr images ls, pull that blob out of the content store, and find yourself reading the same index JSON your own parser printed — same media type, same 16 entries, same eight unknown/unknown attestation manifests you learned to skip in I4.6.

    One detail to notice in passing, because I7.13 is built on it: the blob listing carries containerd.io/gc.ref.content.* labels. Those are the reference graph, written down. The index blob for a multi-platform image carries one gc.ref label per manifest it points at — sixteen of them for alpine:3.21 — and that is literally how the collector knows the manifests are still wanted.

    containerd stores the relationships as labels because the blobs themselves are opaque to it — the client, not the daemon, understands what an image is:

    an OCI image has a manifest which references a config file and layer tars. These resources are stored in containerd as generic blobs, it is the client that understands the relationships between these blobs and sets them up using labels on the content resources.

    I7.8

    Sixty-eight plugins in one binary, and a config file that isn't there

    Why it matters

    ctr plugins ls is the fastest triage command on a container host, and almost nobody knows it exists. It tells you, in one screen, which of containerd's ~68 internal components loaded, which were skipped, and which failed — so "containerd is up but nothing works" resolves in seconds instead of a journal-reading session. This checkpoint also settles what containerd's configuration file actually is, which is not what most people assume.

    The concept

    containerd is one binary, but internally it is a registry of plugins that register themselves at start-up and declare dependencies on each other. "Plugin" here almost never means a file you installed — the snapshotters, the differs, the gRPC services and the CRI implementation are all compiled in. What the plugin model buys is ordering (a service starts only after what it needs) and per-component failure (a broken CRI configuration leaves one plugin in error state while the rest of the daemon runs happily).

    That last property is the reason to look. A daemon that is "up" can be missing exactly the piece you need, and the plugin table says which.

    Each plugin has a type and an id. On this box there are 68 of them, and the type histogram is a fair description of what containerd is:

    TypeCountWhat they are

    I7.9

    The content store, which you already built once

    Why it matters

    You built this store already. In I5.14 pull.py saved each downloaded blob to a file named after its digest, and you verified the download by hashing the file and comparing. containerd's content store is that same idea with an API bolted on and nothing else added — so the first time you look at it, you should recognise it. What you gain here is the ability to answer "are the bytes actually on this node?" without trusting any tool's opinion.

    The concept

    The content store lives at /var/lib/containerd/io.containerd.content.v1.content/blobs/sha256/, and it holds every blob containerd knows about — manifests, indexes, configs and layer tars, all mixed together, all named by their digest with no sha256: prefix and no extension. It is content-addressed: the name is derived from the bytes, so writing the same blob twice is a no-op and corruption is detectable with one command.

    Look at what is not in that path: there is no namespace in it. This is the physical fact behind I7.5's measurement — the bytes are shared by the whole daemon, while which namespace may see a given blob is recorded in the metadata store (I7.12).

    The files are mode 0444. Nothing, including containerd, rewrites a blob in place; a blob is written once under a temporary ingest name and moved into position when its hash has been verified. That is why the warehouse analogy holds — you never modify a part, you only stock a new part number.

    And that leads to the result worth doing by hand: two interfaces, one store, and they can disagree. ls shows you every blob physically present. ctr content ls shows you every blob your namespace has a reference to. So a file can sit in the directory, hash correctly, and still make ctr content get answer not found — because you asked the index, and the index is per-namespace.

    The image spec defines the digest as the identity of the content, which is the property the whole store rests on:

    The digest property of a Descriptor acts as a content identifier, enabling content addressability. It uniquely identifies content by taking a collision-resistant hash of the bytes.

    I7.10

    From tar files to something you can mount

    Why it matters

    The content store holds tar files. A container needs a mounted filesystem. Something has to turn one into the other, and that something — the snapshotter — is both the largest consumer of disk on a container host and the piece people understand least. It is also the exact place where I4's chain IDs and B5's overlay stack turn out to be the same subject, which is a satisfying moment worth having deliberately.

    The concept

    A snapshotter takes the layer tars from the content store and produces something you can mount. In the room-prep analogy: the flat-pack kit arrives as boxes, and the prep crew stacks B5's transparent sheets into a room you can actually walk into.

    Every snapshot has a key, a parent and a kind. The parents chain upward to a root, which is exactly the layer ordering from I4 — and here is the join: the key of a committed snapshot is I4's chain ID. In I4.12 you computed sha256("<prev> <diff_id>") by hand and found it reproduced containerd's snapshot key. This is that key, in the wild.

    For a single-layer image the recursion has no work to do, so the chain ID is the first diff_id — which makes alpine:3.21 the cleanest possible demonstration: the value in the image config's rootfs.diff_ids[0] and the value in the snapshot's PARENT column are character-for-character identical.

    Physically, each snapshot is a numbered directory under /var/lib/containerd/io.containerd.snapshotter.v1.overlayfs/snapshots/<n>/, containing an fs directory (the content) and, for active snapshots, a work directory (overlayfs's scratch space, exactly as in B5.13). The number is a local counter with no relationship to the digest — the mapping from chain ID to directory number lives in the snapshotter's own small database.

    Two numbers from this box are worth internalising, because they invert most people's intuition: the content store is 691 MB and the snapshotter is 2.2 GB. The unpacked form is roughly three times the compressed form, and it is the snapshotter — not the images you can list — that fills a node's disk.

    Snapshots are containerd's own construction, not an OCI concept. What the image spec does guarantee is the ordering the chain depends on:

    An array of layer content hashes (DiffIDs), in order from first to last.

    and that layers are combined rather than merely unpacked:

    Layer Changesets of media type application/vnd.oci.image.layer.v1.tar are applied, rather than simply extracted as tar archives.

    Chain IDs are a containerd construction on top of exactly that ordering — nothing in the OCI specifications mentions them, which is why you had to derive one by hand in I4 rather than look it up.

    I7.11

    Active and committed, and where your writes physically go

    Why it matters

    "Where does a container's writable layer physically live?" is a question with a real answer, and being able to walk to that directory on the host — and read a file the container wrote a second ago — settles a lot of arguments about how containers work. It is also, immediately, an operational capability: you can retrieve a file from a container that has no shell, no tar, and is too broken to exec into.

    The concept

    Snapshots come in two kinds, and the distinction is the whole design:

    • Committed — finished, immutable, shareable. Every image layer is a committed snapshot, which is why fifty containers from one image cost one copy of it.
    • Active — a scratch layer being written to. Every running container has exactly one, keyed by the container's name rather than a digest.

    Map that onto B5 and there is nothing new to learn: committed snapshots are lowerdir, the active snapshot is upperdir, and the container's root filesystem is the overlay mount of the two. ctr snapshot mounts will print you the exact mount command, and it is the same command you typed by hand in B5.13.

    On this box the counts are 2 active, 56 committed — two running containers, and every image layer they and everything else were built from.

    The consequence people find surprising: a file written inside a container appears immediately in the active snapshot's fs directory on the host, as an ordinary file, readable with cat. There is no copy step and no export. The container was never writing anywhere else — upperdir is where writes land, and the container's view is just that directory stacked on top of read-only ones.

    Equally: the committed parent never changes. Write to a file that exists in a lower layer and overlayfs copies it up first (B5.14); the lower layer's copy is untouched, which is what makes it safe to share.

    The snapshotter interface names the two kinds and the transition between them explicitly:

    A snapshot represents a filesystem state. Every snapshot has a parent, where the empty parent is represented by the empty string. A diff can be taken between a parent and its snapshot to generate a classic layer.
    containerd snapshotter interface — core/snapshots/snapshotter.go

    Gotchas
    • The active snapshot is deleted with the container. Everything in upperdir goes when the container record goes — which is the whole point of a container being ephemeral, and the reason logs and data belong on a volume. Retrieve before you delete.

    • ctr snapshot mounts does not mount anything. It prints a command. The path argument is a placeholder, so passing a real directory does not populate it — a genuine surprise the first time.
    • Multi-layer images have several lowerdir entries, colon-separated and in reverse order — nearest layer first. alpine's single layer hides that; a python:3.13 container will show seven.

    This is the mechanism behind the "get the file out of a broken pod" move that W6.3 reaches for kubectl debug to solve — on a node you can ssh to, the file is simply sitting in the pod container's upperdir.     I7.12

    The third store, and the only one with no upstream

    Why it matters

    There is a third store, it is one file, it is tiny, and it is the only one of the three that cannot be rebuilt. Knowing which of your stores are derivable and which is authoritative is what turns "the node's container state is corrupt" from a panic into a decision — and it is a question a good interviewer will ask you.

    The concept

    containerd's metadata store is a single bbolt file at /var/lib/containerd/io.containerd.metadata.v1.bolt/meta.db. bbolt is an embedded key/value database — the same one etcd is built on, which you met in W1.10. One file, memory-mapped, with buckets nested by namespace.

    It holds everything that is a name or a relationship rather than a byte:

    • image names and the digest each points at
    • container records — spec, snapshot key, labels
    • which namespace may see which blob and which snapshot
    • leases and their expiry

    On this box it is 4.4 MB against the content store's 691 MB and the snapshotter's 2.2 GB — roughly a thousandth of the data, and by far the most valuable thousandth.

    Now the exam-style question the checkpoint sets, which is worth answering carefully because the naive answer is wrong in an interesting way:

    StoreRebuildable?Why
    contentYes, from the registryEvery blob is content-addressed and re-fetchable by digest. Costs bandwidth, loses nothing.
    snapshotterYes, from the content storeCommitted snapshots are unpacked layer tars — pure derivation. Active snapshots are not: they are the containers' writable layers, and those are gone.
    metadataNoThe mapping from names to digests, and the container records themselves, exist nowhere else. There is no upstream to re-fetch a container record from.

    So the answer is "content and snapshots are caches; the metadata store is the source of truth" — with the sharp edge that a running container's writable layer is also unrecoverable, which is a second, independent reason not to treat any of this as disposable.

    One more file to notice: the snapshotter keeps its own small metadata.db at io.containerd.snapshotter.v1.overlayfs/metadata.db — 512 KB here — mapping chain IDs to those numbered directories from I7.10. Two bbolt databases, different jobs, and losing either produces a different mess.

    containerd's own garbage-collection documentation states the daemon's posture toward this data plainly, and it is stricter than most people expect:

    The client is responsible for ensuring that all resources which are created are either used or held by a lease at all times, else they will be considered eligible for removal.
    The containerd daemon has strict resource management and will garbage collect any unused resource.

    • "It is only 4 MB" is exactly backwards. Small does not mean unimportant; it means dense. Every image name and container record on the node is in there.
    • strings is a demonstration, not a tool. Do not build anything on grepping a bbolt file. If you genuinely need to read it, stop containerd and use a bbolt CLI.

    This is the same storage engine as etcd's, and the same reasoning applies at both layers: in W1.10 you learned etcd's MVCC, compaction and defrag because etcd is the cluster's only irreplaceable store. meta.db is the node's irreplaceable store — and unlike etcd, nobody backs it up, which is fine precisely because a node is meant to be replaceable. That equivalence is worth holding onto: it is why draining and rebuilding a node is a routine operation and restoring etcd is not.

    I7.13

    The collector that deletes what nothing references

    Why it matters

    containerd deletes things you did not ask it to delete. That sounds alarming and is actually the correct design, but it produces a genuinely confusing failure the first time you meet it: a blob you fetched a minute ago is simply gone, with no error and no log line you were watching. Understanding the collector — and the one mechanism that holds it off — is what makes the runtime's disk behaviour predictable.

    The concept

    containerd's rule is unusually strict: anything not referenced is eligible for deletion, immediately. There is no grace period by default and no "recently used" heuristic. If nothing points at a blob or a snapshot, it goes.

    References come from two places. Some are structural — a container references its snapshot, an image references its target digest. Others are the containerd.io/gc.ref.content.* labels you already saw in I7.7, which the client writes because only the client knows that a manifest points at a config and some layers. The daemon just follows labels.

    A lease is the escape hatch: a resource that references other resources, so they cannot be collected while it lives. It exists for the window between "I have downloaded this blob" and "I have attached it to something permanent" — during which the blob is, by the rule above, garbage. ctr leases create makes one, and it carries a containerd.io/gc.expire label set 24 hours out, so a client that dies mid-operation cannot leak forever.

    How fast is "immediately"? The collector is a plugin with knobs, and the defaults explain the behaviour exactly: deletion_threshold = 0 (any deletion schedules a collection), mutation_threshold = 100 (or every hundred writes), schedule_delay = '0s'. Measured on this box, an unreferenced blob was gone in under five seconds.

    Two corrections to the usual demonstration, both found by running it. First, ctr content fetch does not leave you with an unreferenced blob — it creates an image record, so the blob is referenced and perfectly safe. To watch a collection you must delete the name afterwards. Second, ctr leases ls immediately after a fetch shows nothing: the temporary lease the operation used was released when it completed, exactly as designed. You cannot catch it by looking afterwards.

    And an honest limitation: ctr leases offers only list, create and delete. There is no flag to perform a fetch under a lease you made — that requires the client library, where client.WithLease(ctx) puts the lease on the context and every call made with it is covered. This is a good early example of the smart-client model from I7.8: the interesting behaviour lives in the client, and ctr is only one client.

    The documentation defines a lease as precisely the "I am not finished with this yet" signal:

    Leases are needed to inform the containerd daemon that a resource may be used in the future after the client completes an operation, even though it currently is not seen as utilized.

    and confirms the 24-hour default you will see in the gc.expire label:

    • The tracker's premise for this checkpoint does not reproduce. ctr content fetch leaves a referenced image, and the lease it used is gone before you can list it. Delete the image name to see a collection — and do not expect to observe a temporary lease after the fact.
    • There is no explicit "run the collector now" command in ctr. You trigger it by causing a deletion, then wait. With schedule_delay = '0s' the wait is seconds, not the thirty often quoted.
    • Deleting a lease can delete data instantly. A lease is the only thing holding some resources; removing it makes them unreferenced, and unreferenced means eligible now. Read ctr leases ls before ctr leases rm.
    • An expired lease is not a deleted lease until the collector runs, so ctr leases ls can briefly show a lease whose gc.expire is in the past. Do not treat its presence as protection.
    • Do not turn the collector off to "be safe". Raising the thresholds on a busy node trades a confusing deletion for a full disk, which is a much worse incident and much harder to diagnose after the fact.

    This collector is not the kubelet's image garbage collection — they are two independent mechanisms with two sets of thresholds, and confusing them wastes a lot of time on a DiskPressure node from W6.12. The kubelet decides which images are unused by Kubernetes and asks CRI to remove them; containerd's collector then reclaims whatever that left unreferenced. An image can be gone from crictl images while its blobs are still on disk for a few seconds, and both tools are telling the truth.

    Docs: containerd's garbage-collection.md — read the gc.ref label table and the lease section together, they only make sense as a pair. Then ctr leases --help, and on the Kubernetes side search for "garbage collection" to find the kubelet's separate imageGCHighThresholdPercent.

    I7.14

    Write access to the socket is root on the node

    Why it matters

    One sentence carries this whole checkpoint: write access to the containerd socket is root on the node. Not "can lead to root", not "with a known exploit" — access to that socket is administrative control of the machine, immediately and by design. Internalising this is what makes you recoil, correctly, from every "just mount the socket into the pod" suggestion you will ever be offered.

    The concept

    Look at what containerd's API is for: it creates processes with arbitrary uids, arbitrary mounts, arbitrary capabilities and arbitrary namespaces. That is the job. It follows, with no cleverness required, that anyone who can ask containerd for a container can ask for a container that mounts the host's root filesystem and runs as uid 0 inside it — and now they can read and write every file on the node.

    There is nothing to patch here, because nothing is broken. A runtime that could not do this would not be a runtime. The socket's 0660 root:root mode is the entire access control, which is why it is owned by root and not group-writable to anyone you would not already trust as root.

    This is B12.2's least-privilege lesson arriving with teeth. The reason the daemonless, rootless build tooling in I6.6 exists at all is to avoid handing this socket — or Docker's equivalent /var/run/docker.sock — to a CI job that only wants to build an image. "Mount the socket so the build can run" and "give this job root on every node it lands on" are the same sentence.

    On a Kubernetes node the consequence is sharper still, and worth being able to state precisely: a pod that can mount the host's container socket needs no RBAC permission at all to become cluster-admin. It does not go through the API server. It reads the kubelet's client certificate off the host disk and simply is the node. Every keycard in W7's authorization model is bypassed, because the attack never knocks on that door.

    This checkpoint has no OCI spec to quote — the socket and its permissions are containerd's, not a standard's — so the reference is containerd's own security posture: the socket is a privileged interface, protected by filesystem permissions and nothing else. The runtime spec does, however, define exactly the capability that makes a mounted host root dangerous:

    rootfsPropagation (string, OPTIONAL) sets the rootfs's mount propagation. Its value is either shared, slave, private or unbindable.

    — i.e. the API openly takes host-mount instructions, because taking them is the point.

    OCI Runtime Specification v1.3.0 — config-linux.md

    You can explain, without using the word "exploit", why socket access equals node root, and why mounting a container socket into a workload is a security decision of the first order rather than a convenience.

    Gotchas
    • This is not a containerd flaw, and "fixing" it makes no sense. The same is true of docker.sock, the CRI socket, and any runtime API. The mitigation is never mounting the socket where it does not belong.
    • Group membership is the quiet danger. Adding a user to a docker or containerd-adjacent group is granting passwordless root. It rarely looks like that on the usermod line, which is exactly why it slips through review.
    • A read-only bind mount does not save you. The demo used :ro and still read /etc/shadow; drop the :ro and it writes. Read access to the whole host is already a full compromise — the kubelet's key is world-readable to root.
    • Rootless containerd narrows this but does not erase it. A rootless socket grants the running user's privileges, not root's — better, but still "everything that user can do", which on a build box is often plenty.

    This is the concrete form of B9's note that socket access is node control, and it is why W7's authorization lineup can be entirely sidestepped: a hostPath mount of the socket is a path around RBAC, not through it. The advanced track has no dedicated Pod Security Standards lesson to link here — so the honest pointer is that preventing this is a securityContext/admission concern handled outside these materials, and the rule to carry is simply: the container socket never goes into a container.

    Docs: there is no exam doc for this — it is a habit, not a command. The nearest searchable anchors are the OCI runtime spec's config-linux.md (mounts and propagation) and the Kubernetes docs for "hostPath" and "Pod Security Standards", which exist to stop exactly this.

    I7.15

    The runtime narrating itself

    Why it matters

    ctr events is the runtime's own audit log, streaming live, and it answers the one question node logs are worst at: did this container exit, or was it never started? When a pod is flapping and the journal is ambiguous, watching the event stream while you reproduce the problem tells you the exact sequence and the exact exit code, in order, with timestamps.

    The concept

    Everything you have driven this module is a set of gRPC services over the socket from I7.14: Containers, Tasks, Images, Content, Snapshots, Leases, Events. Every client you have — ctr, nerdctl, the kubelet's CRI calls — is a different face on the same services. ctr events subscribes to the Events service and prints every state change the daemon publishes.

    A single true container produces a whole narrative. Each line is timestamp · namespace · topic · payload, and the topics map one-to-one onto the API calls that caused them. Running one container end to end emits eight:

    /snapshot/prepare→/containers/create→/tasks/create→/tasks/start→/tasks/exit→/tasks/delete→/containers/delete→/snapshot/remove

    That ordering is the whole lifecycle you have spent the module assembling, made observable: the snapshot is prepared before the container record exists, the task is a separate object created and started after the container, and teardown unwinds in reverse. The /tasks/exit event is the one that carries the exit code and the exit timestamp — it is where "container exited 137" physically comes from.

    One honest detail from the capture: a container that exits 0 emits a /tasks/exit event with no exit_status field at all — the field is omitted when it is zero, following protobuf's default-value rule. So "no exit_status in the event" means success, not missing data. A non-zero exit puts the number there.

    An event is a small, typed envelope — and the three fields you read off each line in the lab are exactly the ones the source defines:

    // Envelope provides the packaging for an event.
    type Envelope struct {
      Timestamp time.Time
      Namespace string
      Topic     string
      Event     typeurl.Any
    }

    You can watch a container's lifecycle as an event stream, name the topic that carries the exit code, and read "no exit_status" correctly as "exited zero" rather than "data missing".

    Gotchas
    • Events are per-namespace. On a Kubernetes node you need ctr -n k8s.io events, or you will watch an empty stream while the kubelet is busy — the same -n trap as I7.5, one more time.
    • Events are not persisted. The stream is live only; there is no backlog to query after the fact. If you did not have ctr events running when it happened, it is gone — which is why you start the watcher before reproducing the problem.
    • A restart of containerd breaks the stream silently. Anything that happened during the gap (I7.3) is simply absent, with no marker. Do not treat a quiet stream as "nothing happened".
    • Zero-valued fields vanish, not just exit_status. This is a general protobuf property; do not infer "the runtime didn't record X" from an absent field.

    On a node reproducing a pod-startup problem, ctr -n k8s.io events shows the pause container (W1.21) being created before its app containers, in exactly the order I8's CRI lesson will formalise. When W6.1 teaches kubectl get events as a first move, this is the layer underneath it — Kubernetes events are the cluster's story, containerd events are the node's, and a stuck pod sometimes only shows up in the lower one.

    Docs: containerd's events.md for the topic list and payload types; ctr events --help (there is almost nothing to it). On the Kubernetes side, contrast with kubectl get events --watch, which is the same idea at the cluster layer.

    I7.16

    The friendly client is a face, not a system

    Why it matters

    nerdctl gives you Docker's command-line ergonomics directly on top of containerd, which is genuinely convenient — but the reason it belongs in this module is what it proves: "Docker commands" were never a runtime feature. Everything docker run does is a client-side convention layered on the primitives you have handled by hand, and seeing that is what stops Docker knowledge from misleading you when you debug a cluster.

    The concept

    nerdctl is a Docker-compatible CLI that talks to containerd's ordinary API — the same daemon, the same content store, the same snapshots you have been inspecting. Run a container with it and you can immediately find the identical object with ctr and its shim with ps. There is no second system.

    What nerdctl adds over ctr is convention: default bridge networking, port publishing, named volumes, restart policies, a logs command. Every one of those is a decision, made in the client, on top of primitives that have no opinion — and every one is something Kubernetes chooses to do differently. That is precisely why docker run fluency transfers so unevenly to a cluster: the primitives are shared, the conventions are not.

    One concrete illustration the lab makes visible: nerdctl run --name web does not key the containerd container as web. It generates a 64-hex container ID exactly as Docker does, stores web in a nerdctl/name label, and resolves the friendly name for you at display time. So ctr containers ls shows the long ID, and the name lives in the metadata store (I7.12) as a label — the "friendly name" is pure client sugar, and the container underneath is keyed the way everything in containerd is keyed.

    nerdctl is explicit that it is a client, not a runtime — the same containerd you have been driving:

    Why it matters

    You have driven every one of ctr run's steps separately across this module. Assembling them into one script you wrote yourself is what turns "I read about containerd" into "I can operate containerd" — and it exposes, in a way reading never will, exactly where the friendly one-liner was hiding work.     I7.18

    Three faults that each look like a dead container

    Why it matters

    Three faults that each look, at first, like a dead container — and each has a completely different cause, diagnosis and repair. Telling them apart from the evidence alone is the skill this whole module was building toward, and two of the three behave differently on containerd 2.x than the folklore (and this repo's own tracker) predicts. You will meet all three on real nodes.

    The concept

    Work each fault from evidence to repair. Do not read ahead to the next one until you can explain the current one to a colleague.

    Fault 1 — containerd restarted under a running container

    The scary-looking one that is actually a non-event. You proved the mechanism in I7.3: containerd is not the workload's parent, the shim is, and the shim survives. Restart the daemon and the workload's PID, start time and elapsed time are unchanged; containerd re-attaches from the state on disk.

    The three-sentence explanation to write out and keep:

    Containerd isn't the parent of your container — a shim process is, and the shim's parent is init. Containerd only talks to that shim over a socket, and it writes down where the socket is, so when it comes back it just reconnects. Nothing ever signalled your process, so there was nothing for it to survive.
    Fault 2 — the shim was killed

    This is where the tracker's premise — "the workload is orphaned and no longer reapable through the API" — is only true for a fraction of a second on containerd 2.x, and running it is the only way to see why.

    Kill the shim and poll the workload every 200 ms. At +200 ms it is exactly as predicted: alive, reparented to PID 1, orphaned. But by +400 ms it is gone — because containerd is watching the shim's ttrpc socket, sees it close, logs shim disconnected then cleaning up dead shim, and reaps the orphan for you. So the workload is not permanently unreapable; containerd's own shim-monitoring cleans it up within about two seconds.

    What actually leaks is the container record. containerd removes the task but not the container, so ctr container ls still shows it — and that is the thing you must remove by hand. The repair is ctr container rm <id>, and if the workload had lingered you would kill it by PID first. This also corrects the older folklore (and I3.14's note about a SIGKILLed runc supervisor): a dead runc supervisor leaves a running container, but a dead shim does not, because containerd is actively monitoring the shim in a way nothing monitors a bare runc.

    Fault 3 — a blob vanished

    A layer you fetched is simply gone, and the cause is I7.13's collector. The diagnosis lives entirely in two commands: ctr content ls shows the blob is no longer referenced, and ctr leases ls shows no lease was protecting it. The bytes left within about five seconds of becoming unreferenced, with no error and no log line you were watching. The repair is not to fix the deletion — the collector was right — but to hold a lease over the window where the blob is not yet attached to anything permanent, or to do the fetch as part of an operation (like a pull that creates an image record) that references it structurally.

      Gone from the index and from the disk, and ctr leases ls confirms nothing was holding it. That empty lease list is the diagnosis.

    Verify

    Given only ctr output, you can distinguish a restarted daemon (nothing wrong), a killed shim (task reaped, container record leaked) and a collected blob (unreferenced, no lease), and you can state the correct repair for each without guessing.

    Gotchas
    • Fault 2's window is real but tiny. If you sleep two seconds before checking, you will conclude the workload "just died" and miss that it was briefly orphaned first. The 200 ms poll is what makes the mechanism visible — a slower look tells a misleading story.
    • The tracker's "orphaned and unreapable" is 1.x-era. Do not carry that belief onto a 2.x node: containerd's shim monitoring reaps the orphan, and your actual job is the leaked container record, not a runaway process. Verify on your own version.
    • Fault 3 leaves no error to grep for. The deletion is correct behaviour, so nothing logs a warning. If you go looking for an error you will not find one — the evidence is the absence in ctr content ls plus the empty ctr leases ls, not any message.
    • Deleting files from the content store by hand is a fourth fault, not a repair. It produces a pull that succeeds (the metadata still references the blob) and a container that will not start (the bytes are gone) — the one from I7.9's gotcha. Never treat the store as a directory to clean up manually; that is the collector's job.

    Fault 1 is why W6.11's kubelet fault-hunting lets you restart containerd mid-investigation without fear. Fault 2 is the node-level shape of a stuck-Terminating pod (W6.18) — a leaked record that outlives its process. Fault 3 is the runtime half of the image-pull failures in W6.17: "the layer was here and now it is gone" has a specific, diagnosable cause, and it is not the registry's fault.

    Docs: containerd's garbage-collection.md (fault 3) and runtime-v2.md (the shim lifecycle behind fault 2); journalctl for reading containerd's own account of a shim death. On the Kubernetes side, search "restart kubelet" and "container runtime" to see how each fault surfaces one layer up.

    Quiz

    1. You run a container, then ps -ef shows no runc process anywhere. Is something wrong?
      Answer

      No — that is the healthy state. runc create and runc start both exit as soon as they have done their work (I3.5), so on a running node there are normally zero runc processes. What holds the container open is the shim, not runc. Several stuck runc processes would be the interesting case: a container wedged mid-creation.

    2. The shim is the workload's parent. What is the shim's parent, and why does the answer matter?
      Answer

      PID 1 (systemd). containerd starts the shim detached, so it is immediately re-parented to init — and therefore containerd is an ancestor of nothing. That is exactly why restarting containerd kills no workloads (I7.3): the daemon is never in the process ancestry of the thing it manages.

    3. You systemctl restart containerd under a running container. What changes and what does not?
      Answer

      containerd's PID changes (it is a new process). The workload's PID, start time and elapsed time do not change — it was never signalled. The new containerd re-attaches to the shim by reading init.pid and the socket from bootstrap.json on disk. Measured on this box: containerd 2805 → 56260, workload steady at PID 54899 with 02:36 elapsed.

    4. On a fresh Kubernetes node, ctr images ls shows nothing. Is the node broken?
      Answer

      Almost certainly not — you are in the wrong containerd namespace. The CRI plugin puts everything in k8s.io; ctr defaults to default. Add -n k8s.io. And ctr will silently invent a namespace you mistype, returning an empty table with exit 0, so a typo produces the same symptom.

    5. You pull an image into a brand-new containerd namespace and the progress bar reports 3.5 MiB transferred. How many new blob files hit the disk?
      Answer

      Zero, if the blobs were already present. Namespaces separate names and metadata, not bytes — the content store on disk has no namespace in its path. The "3.5 MiB" is the image's declared size, not bytes moved. Measured: 472 → 472 blob files, 34 new metadata rows.

    6. What is the difference between a containerd container and a task, and what leaks if you confuse them?
      Answer

      A container is metadata (spec, image ref, snapshot key); a task is a running process created from it. Deleting a task but forgetting the container leaves the container record referencing its active snapshot forever — a silent disk leak that ctr task ls cannot show you, because it lists tasks and there is no task. ctr container ls and ctr snapshot ls find it.

    7. An image's config lists one diff_id, and its container's snapshot shows a PARENT digest. Why are they identical here but not in general?
      Answer

      The snapshot's parent key is the layer's chain ID (I4.12), computed by the recursion sha256("<prev> <diff_id>"). For a single-layer image the recursion terminates immediately, so chain ID == the first diff_id. A multi-layer image's top chain ID is a hash of the whole stack and matches none of the individual diff_ids.

    8. You have three stores: content, snapshotter, metadata. If you could back up only one, which — and why?
      Answer

      The metadata store (meta.db, ~4.4 MB). Content is re-fetchable from the registry by digest, and committed snapshots are re-derivable from content — both are caches. The name-to-digest mappings and container records exist nowhere else. (Sharp edge: a running container's active snapshot is also unrecoverable, a second thing with no upstream.)

    9. You ctr content fetch a blob, then ctr leases ls to see the lease it created. It shows nothing. Bug?
      Answer

      No — the temporary lease was released the instant the fetch completed, exactly as designed. You cannot observe it after the fact. And the fetch actually created an image record, so the blob is referenced and safe anyway; it will not be collected until you delete the name. The tracker's "fetch without a lease and watch it vanish" does not reproduce for this reason.

    10. Why is write access to /run/containerd/containerd.sock equivalent to root on the node?
      Answer

      Because the runtime's job is to create processes with arbitrary uids, mounts and namespaces. Anyone who can ask for a container can ask for one that bind-mounts / and runs as uid 0 — demonstrated live by reading /etc/shadow through a container. It is not an exploit; it is the design, and it is why the socket must never be mounted into a workload.

    11. You kill a container's shim on a containerd 2.x node. Is the workload now an unreapable orphan?
      Answer

      Only for about 200 ms. The workload is briefly reparented to PID 1, but containerd is monitoring the shim's socket — it logs shim disconnected then cleaning up dead shim and reaps the orphan within ~2 s. What actually leaks is the container record, which you clear with ctr container rm. The "unreapable orphan" belief is 1.x-era.

    12. A layer blob was on the node a minute ago and is now gone, with no error logged. What happened, and how do you prove it?
      Answer

      The garbage collector reclaimed it after it became unreferenced (its image name was removed) — correct behaviour, so nothing logs a warning. The evidence is an absence: the digest is missing from ctr content ls and ctr leases ls is empty, proving nothing held it. A lease over the vulnerable window would have saved it.
